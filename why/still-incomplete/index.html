Why this exists · Second edition

Why it’s still incomplete.

Adam Overberg · 31 October 2026 · Read the first edition

When I wrote the first edition of this page, districtry covered most of Illinois. It now works in 17 states and San Francisco. You pick a spot on the map, and it tells you which districts contain it and who represents you in each one.

None of those maps is finished. The site keeps a record of every piece it is missing: what is missing, why, and what it would take to fill it. As of today there are 236 of those records, and when I read through all of them, very few describe a technical problem. Of the 236, 146 are things I haven’t found published anywhere. Another 68 are published but out of date, incomplete, or in a form a computer can’t read, and 22 are published somewhere I can’t get to.

I want to be careful about what that means. Everything here is what I’ve been able to find, or what people have given me. If I couldn’t find something, that doesn’t mean it isn’t out there, and if you know where it is, please tell me. But after reading all 236, a few patterns are hard to miss.

The most common reason a spot on the map is blank is that, as far as I can tell, nobody keeps the list. Take city halls. In Arkansas, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee and Iowa, I haven’t found a state office that publishes who runs each city and town. I read the largest cities’ own websites one at a time, but that still leaves nearly 3,000 smaller cities and towns in those six states where the map can name the place and not its mayor or council. School boards are much the same: in at least 10 of the 17 states, the map names your school district but nobody on its board, because I haven’t found a statewide list of members I can read. In New York I haven’t found a state list of county board members, so in 48 of the 57 counties outside New York City the map names nobody. Each New York fire district elects five commissioners, and I haven’t found anything the county or the state publishes that says who they are. I only count a special district as covered when its board members are named, and that keeps much of the map blank on purpose.

Sometimes the people are published but the lines aren’t. In 57 of Mississippi’s 82 counties, I can name all five supervisors but can’t show which one represents a given spot. In 56 of Louisiana’s 64 parishes, I can name the police jury or parish council members and their district numbers, but I have no map of where those districts run. In 76 of Missouri’s 114 counties, and in the City of St. Louis, I can name the commission but can’t say which member represents you. In North Carolina, county commissioner districts are kept as written descriptions in the county clerk’s office rather than as maps.

Sometimes what’s published is an election result. In Minnesota, South Carolina and 31 Michigan counties, I haven’t found anyone who publishes who holds each county seat today. The results say who won. They don’t say who has resigned or been appointed since.

Some of what I need is published, but I’ve been asked not to read it. Some websites ask automated readers to stay out, and I respect that. Others put up a check that only a person in a browser can pass, and I don’t try to get around it. The court systems in Michigan, Minnesota, North Carolina, Oklahoma and Tennessee keep their lists of judges closed to automated readers, and twelve Michigan counties’ websites turn them away. Rochester Hills, Michigan publishes a complete council page, with a phone number and email for every member, and asks automated readers not to read it. Several tribal nations’ websites sit behind a browser check, so their councils aren’t named.

Some of it is for sale, and I don’t buy data. Champaign and Piatt counties in Illinois are behind a GIS consortium’s licence, and a Missouri county quoted about $300 per map layer. The League of Wisconsin Municipalities keeps the only statewide list of Wisconsin’s city and village council members that I know of, and told me it can’t be reused.

And sometimes two official sources disagree. In Kentwood and Ann Arbor, Michigan, the city’s ward lines don’t match another government’s. In the Bronx, two city and court sources name different county clerks. When that happens, I don’t pick one.

I could make the map look complete quickly. I could draw old district lines, list last election’s winners as if they still serve, or guess. But on the map a guess looks exactly like a fact, and old lines could name the wrong person for a lot of people. So I leave the blanks, and each one says why it’s blank.

Mostly, what I do about it is ask. On 1 October I sent 36 letters in one afternoon, and eight were answered within the hour. In late October I wrote to 60 Missouri counties, and the map now draws commission districts in 38 of them, several from maps that clerks sent back. I’ve asked the Illinois State Board of Elections for one statewide precinct map, and written to state offices in Louisiana and Mississippi about their district lines. And I keep adding states.

Asking one office at a time works, but slowly. I think most of these gaps would close if each state published three things, kept current, in one place: who holds each elected seat today, not only who won it; the lines each seat is elected from, as a map file a computer can read rather than a PDF, a scan or a written description; and permission to reuse both for free, so anyone can build on them.

Much of this probably already exists inside state government, since an election office has to know every contest and every precinct to print the right ballots. If that’s true, publishing it may be more a choice than a technical problem.

From what I’ve seen, most blanks look like a list nobody was asked to keep.
